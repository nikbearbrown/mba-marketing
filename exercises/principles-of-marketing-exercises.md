## Chapter 01: Chapter 1 — Setting the Stage
*Source: `chapters/01-setting-the-stage.md`*

## Exercises

### Warm-Up

**1.** The value ratio is $V = B/P$. A streaming service raises its monthly price from $9.99 to $15.99 but adds a library of 500 new films. Has customer value gone up, down, or is it impossible to say? Explain what additional information you would need to answer with confidence. *(Tests: understanding that $B$ is perceived, not actual; that the ratio moves on both sides simultaneously.)*

**2.** Name three decisions a hospital makes that are marketing decisions under the definition in this chapter — even though no one in the hospital calls them that. For each, identify which of the four Ps it belongs to. *(Tests: that marketing decisions are defined by their structure, not by who makes them.)*

**3.** A firm lowers its price by 20%. Under what conditions does this increase customer value? Under what conditions does it decrease it? *(Tests: the price-quality signal embedded in $P$, and the possibility that lowering $P$ can lower $B$.)*

<!-- → TABLE: Student answer scaffold for Exercise 3 — two-column table: Condition / Effect on V. Four rows: (1) price drop perceived as discount with quality unchanged, (2) price drop perceived as quality reduction, (3) price drop that removes friction from the purchase, (4) price drop in a luxury category where price IS the signal. Student should see that the same lever move produces opposite effects depending on context. -->

### Application

**4.** Pick one product you purchased in the last month — anything from a coffee to a software subscription to a piece of clothing. Map it onto the value equation: what was in your $B$ bundle (list at least three distinct benefits, functional and non-functional), and what was in your $P$ (list every cost beyond money). Then identify which of the four Ps the firm used most aggressively to win your purchase. *(Tests: ability to decompose $B$ into a bundle; ability to see $P$ as more than price.)*

**5.** Two gyms operate in the same neighborhood. One charges $25/month with basic equipment and no classes. The other charges $120/month with premium equipment, group classes, and a juice bar. Both are profitable and have been operating for ten years. Using the value ratio and the four Ps, explain why neither has driven the other out of business. *(Tests: that different $B/P$ configurations can coexist when they serve different customers.)*

**6.** The chapter argues that "engineering optimizes the actual product; marketing optimizes the relationship between the product and the person's perception of it." Give one example where these two goals align perfectly and one example where they create tension inside a firm. *(Tests: understanding the distinction between actual and perceived benefits, and the organizational friction that can create.)*

### Synthesis

**7.** A startup founder tells you: "We have the best product in our category. We just need to get the word out." Using the framework from this chapter, identify everything that could be wrong with that sentence — not as a critique of the founder, but as a map of what they haven't yet considered. *(Tests: integration of $B/P$ ratio with the four Ps; the distinction between quality and perceived quality; the question of which customer's $B$ is being optimized.)*

**8.** The chapter introduces three units: Foundation, Audience, Levers. A firm launches a product, finds it's not selling, and immediately invests in advertising (Unit 3 lever). Based on the architecture described in this chapter, what Unit 1 and Unit 2 questions should the firm have answered first — and what would each answer have changed about the advertising decision? *(Tests: understanding why the book is sequenced the way it is; ability to reason about upstream decisions and downstream consequences.)*

### Challenge

**9.** Choose a company that has failed in the last ten years — one you know well enough to have an opinion about. Reconstruct its failure using only the vocabulary from this chapter: the value ratio, the four Ps, and the three-unit sequence (Foundation, Audience, Levers). Which part of the framework did they get wrong first? What happened downstream as a result? Limit yourself to 400 words. *(Tests: ability to apply the framework analytically to a real case; distinguishes students who understood the machinery from students who memorized the terms.)*

## Chapter 02: Chapter 2 — Marketing and Customer Value
*Source: `chapters/02-marketing-and-customer-value.md`*

## Exercises

### Warm-up

**1. Decode a purchase you made this week.** Pick something you bought recently — coffee, a piece of clothing, an app subscription, anything. Write one sentence for each of the four value layers (functional, monetary, social, psychological) describing what that layer contributed to your decision. Then identify which single layer did the most work. Was it the obvious one? If not, what does that tell you about how the product is marketed?

*Tests: the four-layer model of $B$. Difficulty: Low.*

**2. Identify the need level.** For each of the following customer statements, name the highest need level being expressed — stated, real, unstated, delight, or secret — and explain your reasoning in one sentence.

- "I just want a gym bag that holds my stuff."
- "I bought the Yeti because it keeps my coffee hot for six hours."
- "I got the same running shoes the elites use."
- "I didn't expect the app to remember my preferences between sessions — that was a nice surprise."

*Tests: the five-level need framework. Difficulty: Low.*

**3. Name the marketing orientation.** In two to three sentences each, identify which marketing concept — production, product, sales, marketing, or societal — best describes each of the following firms, and say what evidence in the description points there.

- A craft brewery that releases whatever the head brewer finds interesting, keeps no tap handles in chain restaurants, and expects customers to seek them out.
- A software company that cold-emails 10,000 small businesses per week with the same pitch regardless of whether they have expressed interest.
- A consumer electronics brand that starts every product cycle by interviewing customers about unmet frustrations, then builds to address the top-ranked one.

*Tests: the evolution of marketing concepts. Difficulty: Low.*

### Application

**4. Run $V = B/P$ on a real competition.** Choose two products competing in the same category — two streaming services, two protein bars, two project management tools. For each product, estimate $B$ on a 0–100 scale by scoring all four value layers (assign weights if you like). Look up or estimate $P$. Compute $V = B/P$ for each.

Does the higher-$V$ product have the higher market share? If yes, the model predicted the outcome. If no, what is the model missing — a value layer, a distribution advantage, a switching-cost barrier? Name it specifically.

*Tests: $V = B/P$ as a predictive tool; forces confrontation with the model's limits. Difficulty: Medium.*

**5. Find the broken mix.** Identify a product whose four Ps are telling inconsistent stories — a premium-priced item sold in a discount channel, an aspirational brand running price-led advertising, a mass-market product in boutique-only distribution. Document the specific inconsistency: which P contradicts which other P, and how would a customer experience the contradiction? Then propose a fix — not "align the Ps" but a specific change to one or two levers that would restore coherence.

*Tests: the 4Ps as a system, not a checklist. Difficulty: Medium.*

**6. Scan one factor that dominates.** Pick a brand operating in a category where the macroenvironment is visibly shifting — fast fashion, ride-sharing, print media, electric vehicles. Run a PESTLE scan, write one sentence per factor, then cross out the four factors that are currently background noise and circle the one or two that are actually constraining what the brand can do right now. For each circled factor: is the brand's current marketing running with the change or against it?

*Tests: the three-environment framework; breaks the fill-every-box PESTLE habit. Difficulty: Medium.*

### Synthesis

**7. Connect the environments to the mix.** Apply the three-environment framework to the JanSport × *Stranger Things* collaboration. For the internal environment: which of the 5Ms was the most critical resource JanSport needed to execute the collaboration, and which was likely the most constrained? For the microenvironment: which single actor outside JanSport's control most determined whether the collaboration succeeded or failed? For the macroenvironment: which PESTLE factor best explains why branded entertainment collaborations became a viable marketing strategy at all?

Then draw the chain: trace how one environment factor you identified directly shaped one specific P in JanSport's mix. Show the link explicitly.

*Tests: integration of the environment model with the 4Ps. Difficulty: Medium-high.*

**8. Apply the ethics boundary.** A fitness supplement brand wants to run advertising claiming its product is "the most trusted pre-workout in America" and "clinically proven to improve performance." Evaluate each claim: is it puffery, a verifiable factual claim, or a potentially false claim? What would the brand need to be able to demonstrate to defend each claim if a competitor brought a Lanham Act challenge?

Now write two alternative taglines that make the strongest honest case for the product's $B$ without crossing the legal or ethical line. For each, identify which need level — stated, real, unstated, delight, or secret — the tagline is targeting.

*Tests: ethics as a $B$ constraint, not a separate topic; Lanham Act; need-level identification. Difficulty: Medium-high.*

### Challenge

**9. Build the full picture for a product that fails.** Find a product that has genuinely struggled — declining sales, failed launch, loss of market share. Reconstruct what went wrong using all three frameworks from this chapter: $V = B/P$ (which layer of $B$ eroded, or did $P$ move without a corresponding $B$ move?), the 4Ps (was there a consistency failure in the mix?), and the three environments (did a macroenvironment shift make the product's previous $B$ irrelevant?). Use verifiable public information — sales data, press coverage, the company's own statements.

The goal is not a list of problems. It is one coherent explanation: here is the root cause, here is how the three frameworks each see it, and here is what a different marketing decision at a specific moment would have changed.

*Tests: synthesis of all three frameworks in a real-world diagnostic. Difficulty: High.*

## Chapter 03: Chapter 3 — Strategic Planning in Marketing
*Source: `chapters/03-strategic-planning-in-marketing.md`*

## Exercises

### Warm-Up

**1. The SMART test.** For each objective below, identify which SMART criterion it fails most badly, then rewrite it to pass all five:

- "Grow our social media presence this year."
- "Increase revenue in the enterprise segment."
- "Become the most trusted brand in our category by Q3."
- "Reduce customer churn."

*(Tests: ability to apply SMART as a diagnostic, not just recite the acronym. Difficulty: Low.)*

**2. Name the growth strategy.** A mid-size athletic apparel brand is considering four initiatives. For each, name which of the four growth strategies it represents (market penetration, product development, market development, product diversification) and explain in one sentence why:

- Launching a children's clothing line sold to the same retail partners.
- Running a loyalty-points program to increase repeat purchases from existing customers.
- Entering the Brazilian market with the existing product line.
- Acquiring a sports nutrition company and selling protein bars under the apparel brand.

*(Tests: recognition of strategy types in slightly disguised form. Difficulty: Low.)*

**3. BCG placement.** A firm sells four products. Annual revenue and estimated market growth rates are: Product A — $420M, 2% market growth; Product B — $85M, 18% market growth; Product C — $310M, 1% market growth; Product D — $40M, 22% market growth. The firm is the market leader in A and C, holds roughly 8% share in B (leader holds 30%), and holds 6% share in D (leader holds 25%).

Place each product in the BCG matrix. For each, name the prescribed strategic action. *(Tests: ability to apply the classification logic, not just name the quadrants. Difficulty: Low-medium.)*

<!-- → TABLE: Student answer scaffold for Exercise 3 — five columns: Product, Market Growth, Firm's Share, Quadrant, Prescribed Action. Pre-filled with product labels; student fills in the rest. Reinforces the two-axis classification logic before the student attempts the full exercise. -->

### Application

**4. Compute and interpret the KPIs.** A firm reports the following data: revenue this year $2.3 billion, revenue last year $1.9 billion; total category size $38 billion; leading competitor's revenue $5.1 billion.

Compute: year-over-year revenue growth; the firm's market share; the leading competitor's market share; relative market share (firm's share ÷ leader's share).

Then write one paragraph — no more than 150 words — interpreting the firm's strategic position. What does the combination of these four numbers tell you that any one of them alone would not? *(Tests: paired-metric discipline; ability to read a strategic situation from numbers rather than anecdote. Difficulty: Medium.)*

**5. CLV segment decision.** Use the four-segment CLV table from the chapter. A product team proposes investing $300,000 to improve onboarding for Gen Z customers, with the goal of increasing their average retention from 2 years to 4 years.

Compute the revised Gen Z CLV. Then answer: does this investment change the segment priority ranking? By how much per customer does the ranking change? What retention length would Gen Z need to achieve to surpass Millennial singles as the highest-CLV segment? *(Tests: CLV arithmetic and the discipline of asking "what would have to be true" before committing to a strategic bet. Difficulty: Medium.)*

**6. Read a real SWOT.** Find a published SWOT analysis for any real firm — a case study, an investor presentation, an MBA report, or a news article that frames things this way. Identify two problems with how it was constructed. Common failure modes: items misclassified as internal when they're external (or vice versa); no prioritization within quadrants; vague items that wouldn't drive any decision ("we have talented employees").

Rewrite one quadrant to fix the problems you found. *(Tests: SWOT as a decision tool rather than a worksheet. Difficulty: Medium.)*

### Synthesis

**7. Strategy chain stress test.** Pick a firm you know well enough to have an opinion about — a company you've worked for, a brand you follow closely, or a business from a previous case study. Write:

- One vision statement (specific enough to constrain at least one type of decision)
- One mission statement (specific enough to say what the firm won't do)
- Three SMART objectives for the coming year
- One growth strategy (which of the four?) with a one-paragraph justification

Then check the chain: does the growth strategy follow from the objectives? Do the objectives follow from the mission? Would a competitor reading the mission know something the firm is *not* going to do?

If the chain breaks anywhere, fix it. Note where the break was and what fixing it required. *(Tests: constraint propagation across the strategy chain. Difficulty: Medium-high.)*

**8. The measurement trap.** A firm's marketing team sets the following KPI for the year: "increase website traffic by 40 percent." By December, traffic is up 43 percent. The team declares success.

But the sales team reports that conversion rates dropped from 3.2 percent to 1.8 percent. Revenue is flat.

Using the frameworks in this chapter, explain what went wrong at the level of KPI design. What metric was the team optimizing for instead of the outcome they actually wanted? Redesign the measurement plan: propose three KPIs that, together, would have caught this problem before December. For each KPI, name the gaming risk — the way a team could hit the number without producing the underlying outcome. *(Tests: the distinction between a metric and a KPI; the anti-gaming discipline of leading + lagging indicator pairing. Difficulty: Medium-high.)*

### Challenge

**9. Strategy theatre autopsy.** Find the public strategic language of a firm you believe is doing strategy theatre — a mission statement, an annual report summary, or a "our strategy" section from a corporate website. Copy three specific phrases.

For each phrase: explain what decision the phrase was supposed to constrain, then describe what the firm actually did in the relevant domain. If the phrase failed to constrain any decision, rewrite it in one sentence so that it would.

The win condition is not identifying that the language is vague — that's easy. The win condition is being specific about *what choice was being avoided* and *what would happen to the firm if it made that choice explicit*. *(Tests: reading strategy critically rather than reverently. Difficulty: High.)*

## Chapter 04: Chapter 4 — Understanding the Marketplace
*Source: `chapters/04-understanding-the-marketplace.md`*

## Exercises

### Warm-up

**1. Crack the "everyone" problem.** Choose a product whose marketing seems to target "everyone" — a major airline, a large grocery chain, a popular streaming platform, a smartphone from any of the top three brands. Write three short customer sketches (two to three sentences each) describing three genuinely different people who could plausibly buy this product. For each sketch, name the one thing they care about most in the purchase decision — the variable that would most influence their $B$ calculation.

Now ask: if those three people have such different priorities, what does it mean for the firm to claim it serves all three? Is that a strategy or a default? What would it cost the firm to choose one and build for them specifically?

*Tests: the core argument of the chapter — "aimed at everyone is aimed at no one." Difficulty: Low.*

**2. Name the right segmentation variable.** The chapter argues that dividing the outdoor gear market by age would probably produce useless segments, while dividing by *how the gear is used* would likely produce useful ones. For each of the following markets, propose one segmentation variable that is probably useless and one that is probably useful. In one sentence each, explain why.

- A bank offering personal checking accounts
- A meal-kit delivery service
- A fitness apparel brand

*Tests: the definition of a meaningful segment — similar needs inside, different needs outside. Difficulty: Low-medium.*

### Application

**3. Audit your own assumptions.** Think of a product, brand, or business you know well — something you have worked for, a family business, a brand you use daily and have strong opinions about. Write the customer description you would give if someone asked "who is your customer?" right now.

Then run the audit question from the chapter against it: *what am I assuming about this customer that I have not verified?* List at least three specific assumptions embedded in your description. For each assumption, name what evidence would confirm or refute it — a specific type of research, a dataset, an observable behavior.

*Tests: the empirical discipline of customer knowledge — the distinction between a hypothesis and a verified claim. Difficulty: Medium.*

**4. Explain the order to someone who disagrees.** A classmate argues that it makes more sense to start with segmentation (Chapter 7) before studying buyer behavior (Chapters 5–6) — "you need to know which customers you're after before you waste time understanding how they think."

Write a two-paragraph response that explains, using the chapter's logic, why this sequence gets the answer wrong even if it feels efficient. Be specific: what would go wrong in the segmentation if you had not first understood decision mechanisms?

*Tests: the chapter's core argument about prerequisite ordering. Difficulty: Medium.*

### Challenge

**5. Find the segmentation theater.** The chapter names a specific failure mode in diverse-market marketing: firms that visually represent diversity in their advertising while leaving the underlying product, value proposition, and channel strategy unchanged.

Find a real example — a campaign, a product launch, a rebranding — where you believe this failure mode occurred. Describe the evidence: what did the marketing signal, and what did the product, price, or channel signal instead? Then describe what *genuine* marketplace knowledge would have looked like in this case — not better photography, but a different customer understanding that would have produced different decisions upstream of the creative.

*Tests: the distinction between representation and knowledge; applies the chapter's argument about global and diverse markets to a real case. No fabrication — use documented examples and label any inference as inference. Difficulty: High.*

## Chapter 05: Chapter 5 — Consumer Markets and Purchasing Behavior

## Exercises

### Warm-Up

**1. Classify five purchases.** List the last five things you bought, regardless of size or category. For each, name (a) the type of buying behavior — complex, dissonance-reducing, habitual, or variety-seeking — and (b) which of the five influence categories was most dominant. Where you're genuinely unsure, record the uncertainty rather than forcing a clean answer.

*(Tests: Objectives 2, 3. Difficulty: Low. Forces application of both frameworks to lived experience before they feel abstract.)*

**2. Walk a real decision through the five stages.** Take your decision to attend your current college — or a major purchase you made in the last year that involved real deliberation. Write one to two sentences for each of the five stages, describing what specifically happened. Be honest about which stages you skipped or collapsed into seconds, and name the heuristic, if any, that closed Stage 4 for you.

*(Tests: Objective 4. Difficulty: Low.)*

**3. Match the campaign to Maslow.** For each of the following campaigns, identify which level of Maslow's hierarchy is being targeted and explain in one sentence why:

- **Geico:** *"15 minutes could save you 15 percent or more on car insurance."*
- **De Beers:** *"A diamond is forever."*
- **Apple:** *"Think different."*
- **Lysol:** *"Practice Healthy Habits."*

*(Tests: Objective 5. Difficulty: Low.)*

### Application

**4. Identify the dominant heuristic.** Choose three product categories where you have made a purchase in the last six months. For each, identify which heuristic most consumers in that category likely use at Stage 4 — *price = quality*, *brand loyalty*, *country of origin*, *most reviews*, *highest rating*, or another you observe. Then identify one specific consumer segment for whom a *different* heuristic would dominate, and explain why.

**5. Diagnose a perceptual failure.** Find a marketing campaign you remember from the last six months but cannot name the brand for. Then propose, in two to three sentences, one specific change to the campaign that would have corrected it.

*(Tests: Objective 6. Difficulty: Medium.)*

**6. Map the four quadrants in a category you know.** Pick a product category you understand well — gaming hardware, athletic footwear, restaurant choice, coffee shops, streaming services, anything. For each of the four buying-behavior types, write a one-paragraph profile of what a consumer in that quadrant looks like in your chosen category. Then write one sentence describing how the marketing mix should differ across the four quadrants.

*(Tests: Objective 2. Difficulty: Medium. Forces system-level application of the involvement × differentiation framework, not just label recognition.)*

**7. Map the hip-hop/sneaker mechanism to a different category.** The chapter tracked how hip-hop subculture reshaped the sneaker category from 1986 onward. Document at least three specific brand-collaboration or cultural-endorsement moments that show the mechanism in action. For each, name which of the five influence categories was doing the primary work.

**8. Diagnose the Pepsi paradox.** Pepsi reliably won blind taste tests against Coca-Cola. Coca-Cola did not lose market share in the way those results would have predicted. Write a 250-word explanation of why, using at least one mechanism from each of the chapter's three main sections — the five categories of influence, the five-stage decision process, and the psychological mechanisms (Maslow, perception, beliefs/attitudes). Do not repeat the chapter's own explanation verbatim; apply the frameworks to reconstruct the reasoning yourself.

*(Tests: Objectives 1, 4, 6. Difficulty: Medium-high. Forces integration across the chapter's three frameworks.)*

### Challenge

**9. The ethics positioning audit.** Find a brand that explicitly competes on ethical consumerism — Patagonia, Allbirds, TOMS, Beyond Meat, Lush, Ben & Jerry's, or anyone with a visible ethics-as-positioning claim. Conduct a structured audit: (a) what specific ethical claims does the brand make publicly, (b) what evidence does it publish to support those claims, (c) what would a hostile critic point to as a credibility gap, (d) how would the brand's positioning need to evolve if that credibility gap became a public story.

Then evaluate: is the brand's ethics positioning *durable* — hard to displace because the operations genuinely back it — or *fragile* — dependent on consumers not looking too closely? What would make the difference?

*(Tests: Objective 8. Difficulty: High. Tests evaluative judgment about the relationship between ethics, evidence, and brand-positioning durability.)*

## Chapter 06: Chapter 6 — Business Markets and Purchasing Behavior
*Source: `chapters/06-business-markets-and-purchasing-behavior.md`*

## Exercises

### Warm-up

**1. Classify the transaction.** For each of the following, identify whether the transaction is B2C or B2B. If B2B, name the buyer type (producer, reseller, government, institution) and identify whether the demand is direct or derived.

- A dental school buys disposable gloves by the case from a medical supply distributor.
- A consumer buys running shoes from Nike's website.
- A sneaker retailer buys 500 pairs of running shoes from Nike's wholesale division.
- A chip manufacturer buys silicon wafers to produce processors that go into laptops.
- A city government contracts a firm to resurface 40 miles of road.

*Tests: Objectives 1, 2, 7. Difficulty: Low.*

**2. Name the buying-center role.** The following people are involved in a hospital's purchase of a new patient-scheduling software system. For each, identify their most likely buying-center role — and note any cases where one person likely holds more than one role.

- The head nurse who filed a formal complaint about the current system's inability to handle shift changes.
- The CFO who must approve any technology spend over $100,000.
- The IT director who will certify whether the new system integrates with existing EHR infrastructure.
- The procurement manager who solicits bids and manages the vendor selection process.
- The department managers and nurses who will use the software every day.
- The CEO's executive assistant who decides which vendor presentations get scheduled.

*Tests: Objective 4. Difficulty: Low.*

**3. Identify the buy class.** For each of the following, identify the buy class (straight rebuy / modified rebuy / new-task buy / systems selling) and name the one marketing implication that matters most given that class.

- A regional restaurant chain that has purchased napkins from the same supplier for six years places its quarterly order.
- A manufacturing company buying industrial robotics equipment for the first time to automate a packaging line.
- A software company purchasing a combined CRM, billing, and project-management platform from a single vendor rather than three separate tools.
- A law firm that has decided to switch copier vendors after the incumbent raised prices by 22 percent at renewal.

*Tests: Objective 3. Difficulty: Low-medium.*

### Application

**4. Trace the derived demand chain.** Pick a consumer product category currently experiencing either strong growth or a notable decline — electric vehicles, streaming video, plant-based food, anything with a visible market trend. Identify three layers of B2B derived demand upstream of the consumer purchase. For each layer, name a plausible firm operating in that derived-demand position and describe the specific mechanism by which the consumer-side trend changes their B2B revenue. What should each of those B2B firms be watching in the consumer market that they don't sell to directly?

*Tests: Objective 7. Difficulty: Medium.*

**5. Map the buying center for a real purchase.** Think of a significant B2B purchase your school, employer, or a company you know has made or is making — a software platform, a facilities contract, a major equipment purchase. Identify who holds each of the six buying-center roles (initiator, influencer, gatekeeper, buyer, decider, user) by job title or description. Where a role is held by multiple people, list them. Where a role is unclear or unknown, say so explicitly rather than guessing.

Then identify which one or two roles you believe most determined the outcome, and explain why.

*Tests: Objectives 4, 5. Difficulty: Medium.*

**6. Match the buying process to the buy class.** For a new-task buy, all eight stages are active. For a straight rebuy, most collapse. For each of the following, identify which of the eight stages were likely compressed or skipped entirely, and explain what organizational logic — not laziness — justifies that compression.

- A hospital reorders the same brand of surgical gloves it has purchased monthly for three years.
- A mid-sized accounting firm purchases its first cloud-based document-management system after outgrowing its shared-drive setup.
- A construction firm re-engages its incumbent concrete supplier for a new project, but negotiates modified delivery terms due to a tighter timeline.

*Tests: Objectives 3, 5. Difficulty: Medium.*

### Synthesis

**7. Reconstruct a failed B2B sale.** Think of a B2B sales situation — one you have been part of, read about, or can reasonably reconstruct from public information — where the vendor lost despite having a competitive product. Apply the buying-center framework: which roles did the vendor reach, and which did it miss? Apply the buy-class framework: which class was the purchase, and did the vendor's approach match? Apply the influence-category framework: which one or two influence categories were most active, and did the vendor's approach address them?

Your answer should not be "the product wasn't good enough." It should identify the specific structural failure — the gatekeeper who was never engaged, the technical influencer who wrote specs the vendor's product couldn't meet, the timing mismatch that put the vendor in a modified-rebuy window when they were selling like it was a new-task.

*Tests: Objectives 4, 5, 6. Difficulty: Medium-high.*

### Challenge

**8. The FCPA scenario.** You are the Southeast Asia regional sales director for a US-based industrial safety equipment manufacturer. A government procurement officer in a country ranked in the bottom quartile of Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index signals that your bid for a $12 million contract will succeed if your firm sponsors a "product training seminar" for the procurement office at a beach resort — a $60,000 event with no clear training agenda.

Your two largest competitors are non-US firms operating under no equivalent legal restriction. Losing this contract means missing your annual target by 30 percent.

Write a 350–400 word analysis covering: (a) whether this constitutes an FCPA violation and what the legal test is, (b) the civil and criminal exposure if you proceed, (c) what specifically you would lose by declining and what you would preserve, (d) whether the competitive disadvantage argument has any legal weight under FCPA, and (e) your recommendation — and what evidence or changed circumstance would change it. No invented facts. If you are uncertain about a specific legal provision, say so and state what you would verify before deciding.

*Tests: Objective 8. Difficulty: High.*
